The case for championsStart with the problem

Licences scale faster
than learning.

Buying access removes one barrier. It does not answer, “How does this help my team do better work?”

As an enterprise grows, the same tool meets different codebases, risk profiles, languages and ways of working. A central team can set direction and provide expertise. It cannot sit beside every team when a new practice meets a real deadline.

A champion program creates an ongoing community of practice: trusted practitioners help peers try useful workflows, share what holds up in practice, and bring recurring blockers back to the people who can fix them. GitHub distinguishes this continuing network from a time-bounded train-the-trainer effort. [1]

When enablement stays centralWhat a supported network can change

One broad training session misses local needs.

Practitioners translate guidance into a team's actual workflow.

The same question keeps reaching the same experts.

Answers become reusable examples; specialists handle the hard cases.

Leaders see usage, but cannot see the friction.

Champions surface patterns, including where tools or policy need to change.

A launch has attention. The next quarter does not.

A regular learning loop keeps knowledge current after launch.

These are mechanisms to test in your organization, not guaranteed productivity gains.

The investment case, without the hype

Fund a small network when repeated questions, uneven adoption or slow feedback are limiting a capability that matters to the business. Tie the pilot to a specific problem, such as helping teams use an approved code-review workflow with fewer repeated support handoffs.

Budget for champion time, coordination, expert support and maintenance of shared material. Compare the results with the baseline and the cost of the network. A program that creates meetings without improving useful work is not succeeding.

Not every organization needs a new program.

If existing communities already connect teams to reliable help and decision-makers, strengthen them. A small, well-connected organization may need a shared channel and protected time, not another layer of governance.

What changes in 2026More capability. More context.

AI raises the stakes
for shared judgment.

A prompt is easy to copy. Knowing when the result is useful, safe and correct takes more work.

For Copilot and agent-assisted work, champions can help teams evaluate outputs, document approved patterns and share failure cases. Keep examples grounded in real tasks with explicit review and data boundaries. A popular workflow is not automatically a safe one.

The 2025 DORA research describes AI as an amplifier of an organization's existing strengths and weaknesses. Its findings support investing in the surrounding system of work, rather than expecting a tool rollout alone to deliver results. The report also identifies delivery-stability risks alongside positive relationships with throughput and product performance. It does not establish that champion programs cause productivity gains. [4]

Share workflows, not just tips.
Capture the task, approved data, steps, human review, observed result and known limitations. Include what failed. Remove obsolete examples when the tool changes.
Keep authority where it belongs.
Security and platform owners set policy, approve access and handle incidents. Champions teach within those boundaries; they cannot waive them.
Make agent permissions explicit.
Use least privilege and approved environments. Review code and consequential actions. Never treat a champion's enthusiasm as authorization to expose data or bypass controls.
Build beyond one product.
The same network can support reusable Actions workflows, platform adoption, inner-source and secure-development practices. Start with one use case; expand only when the network has capacity.

The practices in this section are this guide's recommendations for modern programs, not additional requirements attributed to GitHub or DORA.

The operating modelClear ownership beats extra layers

A network needs
more than volunteers.

Give people permission, time and a route to someone who can make decisions.

GitHub's guidance emphasizes executive sponsorship and enthusiastic, influential practitioners. Advocacy is a behavior, not a title. Recruit for curiosity, generosity and peer trust; high usage alone does not make someone a good champion. [1–3]

Minimum responsibilities. One person may hold more than one role.
WhoOwnsMust not become
Executive sponsorBusiness purpose, protected capacity and removing organizational blockers.A name on a slide with no time or influence committed.
Program ownerThe charter, onboarding, cadence, feedback routing and program reviews. Can be a champion.A new approval bottleneck for everyday learning.
Local championsLearning, peer support, reusable examples and a clear picture of local friction.An unpaid permanent helpdesk or substitute for line management.
Platform & security ownersReliable technical guidance, approved access, policy decisions and escalation response.A disconnected escalation destination that never closes the loop.

Staff for coverage and capacity

Map business units, working hours, languages and relevant disciplines before counting champions. Ask managers what work will stop to make room. Offer an accessible, asynchronous way to participate, not only live sessions at headquarters' convenience.

What do the source templates say about staffing?

The GitHub charter template suggests several hours a week, or about 10% of time, as a starting expectation. It gives context-dependent examples of 1–3 champions per 100 users in smaller organizations and leaner coverage around 1 per 1,000 in larger organizations with tiered lead champions. [2]

Source guidance, not a universal staffing rule. Neither headcount nor a ratio proves adequate coverage. Set capacity against the actual work, geographic spread and escalation load; adjust before volunteers absorb the difference.

A workable learning loop

Use an existing Teams or Slack channel and one searchable home for answers. A possible pilot cadence is weekly asynchronous notes, a fortnightly peer clinic and a monthly sponsor review. This cadence is an editorial starting point; reduce it if it consumes more capacity than it returns. The learning loop comes from the handbook. [3]

Your first 90 daysA suggested pilot, not a deadline

Start narrow.
Earn the next step.

One use case. A representative pilot. A decision at each gate.

The timeline below is this guide's proposed launch path. Adapt it to risk, capacity and procurement constraints. Open each phase for the work, accountable owners and evidence needed to move on.

Days 1–30Days 31–60Days 61–90
Days 1–30Give the pilot a purpose

Accountable: program owner, with sponsor and pilot-team managers.

  • Write a one-page charter: business problem, target teams, approved scope, capacity, escalation owners and review date.
  • Recruit willing, trusted peers across the pilot's working hours. Agree protected time and a rotation or exit path.
  • Capture a baseline for the chosen workflow and collect current blockers. Check applicable policy with platform and security owners.
  • Set up the existing channel, knowledge home and onboarding. Explain what champions can answer and where they escalate.

Leave with: an agreed charter, named people, baseline and one approved workflow to try.

Gate: start hands-on work only when capacity, boundaries and escalation ownership are real. Otherwise narrow or pause the pilot.

Days 31–60Learn in the actual work

Accountable: local champions for peer learning; program owner for resolving feedback.

  • Try the approved workflow on real tasks. Document context, human review, useful results and failures.
  • Run short peer clinics. Turn repeated questions into searchable answers and invite non-users to explain friction.
  • Route policy and platform blockers to their accountable owners. Record decisions and tell teams what changed.
  • Review champion workload with managers. Cut activity that does not help the pilot problem.

Leave with: reviewed examples, a blocker log with owners and evidence of useful repeat use.

Gate: continue when the workflow is useful and reviewable, support requests have an owner, and champion workload fits the agreed capacity. Unresolved safety issues block expansion.

Days 61–90Decide what deserves to scale

Accountable: sponsor and program owner, informed by pilot teams and platform/security owners.

  • Compare the pilot with the baseline. Explain changes in work mix, team size or tooling that complicate attribution.
  • Review adoption alongside quality, support effort and champion wellbeing. Include people who disengaged.
  • Publish a short internal decision: expand, adapt, hold or stop. Explain the evidence and remaining uncertainty.
  • If expanding, onboard the next cohort, close coverage gaps, fund capacity and assign maintenance of shared examples.

Leave with: a sponsor decision, an updated charter, resourcing and the next review date.

Gate: expansion needs useful outcomes, sustainable capacity and working controls. Attendance or licence activation alone is not enough.

Measure what mattersEvidence, not a leaderboard

Activity is a signal.
Useful change is the goal.

Decide what evidence would change your mind before you launch.

The GitHub templates list adoption, engagement, attendance, certifications, contributions and surveys as possible measures. [2] This guide recommends separating those leading signals from outcomes, and checking the cost and risks alongside them.

A measurement menu. Choose a small set for your pilot, not every row.
LensEvidence to collectHow to interpret it
Reach & learningTeams reached, clinic participation, questions answered, reviewed examples contributed.Shows access to help. Does not prove better work.
Useful adoptionRepeat use of the chosen workflow, with team feedback on when it helps and when it does not.Define “repeat” and the eligible population first. Include non-users.
Knowledge & frictionReused answers, recurring blockers, resolution time and handoffs to central support.Look for fewer repeated obstacles, not fewer questions at any cost.
Business & qualityA workflow-specific outcome such as review turnaround, rework or delivery reliability.Compare with baseline and context. Faster is not better if defects rise.
Capacity & wellbeingChampion time, coordinator effort, workload feedback, rotations and uncovered teams.Count the cost. Growing demand without funded time is a warning.

A better sponsor question

“Which team problem got easier, what evidence supports that, what did it cost, and did quality or wellbeing get worse?”

Use aggregated, proportionate evidence. Avoid individual productivity rankings, surveillance and a fabricated ROI number. Observed improvement is not proof that the program caused it.

Keep it sustainableDesign for the quarter after launch

Protect the people
who make it work.

Programs drift when the most helpful people quietly become the support system. Review the network as a service with limits, not as an unlimited supply of goodwill.

Champions become a permanent helpdesk

Separate peer learning from incident response. Publish support boundaries, send repeated problems to an accountable platform owner, and protect agreed capacity. If demand exceeds capacity, reduce scope or fund more support.

The loudest teams get all the attention

Review coverage by business unit, time zone, language and discipline. Offer asynchronous participation. Ask underrepresented teams and non-users what prevents them from joining; do not assume silence means satisfaction.

Recognition rewards volume instead of usefulness

Recognize a clear explanation, a reused answer or a well-escalated risk. Do not rank prompt counts, usage or event attendance. Give managers visibility into the contribution without making participation a hidden performance requirement.

Guidance goes stale as tools evolve

Give shared examples an owner and review date. Re-check them after meaningful tool or policy changes. Archive invalid advice and tell teams what changed. A quarterly charter review is a reasonable starting cadence, not a substitute for responding to a safety issue now.

The program depends on the same few people

Offer graduation, planned rotations and a guilt-free exit. Pair incoming and outgoing champions, preserve the knowledge home and renew manager support. GitHub's recommendation calls for a graduation path so champions can pass the torch, with leadership protecting their time. [1]

Scale the learning.
Not the dependency on a few heroes.
